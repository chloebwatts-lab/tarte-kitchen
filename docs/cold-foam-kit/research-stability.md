# Research Brief: At-Home Cold Foam Product — Foam Science, Stabilizers, Delivery Formats, Shelf Life, QC, Acid-Contact & Flavour Variants

Raw research input for product planning (Tarte, Gold Coast). Context: cold foam sold as part of a "make your own iced black at home" kit — customer brews iced long black / cold brew from Tarte's grind, then tops with the foam. All figures from cited sources; usage rates are % w/w unless noted.

## 1. Foam Physics & Chemistry of Cold Foam

### What cold foam is, structurally
A wet protein-stabilized foam: air bubbles dispersed in an aqueous milk phase, with adsorbed protein films at each air–water interface forming viscoelastic skins. Two distinct stabilization regimes matter for product design:

- **Protein-film foams (low fat, "Starbucks cold foam" style)**: milk proteins adsorb at the interface, lower surface tension, and form a coherent viscoelastic network around bubbles. Foamability is governed by protein adsorption kinetics; stability by interfacial film strength/elasticity ([ScienceDirect — foaming of milk protein dispersions](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S095869462030128X)).
- **Fat-network foams (whipped cream, 30–40% fat)**: stabilized by *partial coalescence* — fat crystals in globules pierce neighboring globules during shear, building a rigid partially-coalesced fat scaffold around bubbles that also traps serum ([ScienceDirect — whipping cream destabilization during static storage](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0268005X22001333)). Requires solid fat (~cold temps; highest partial coalescence with ~60% solid fat content at whipping temperature; tempering at 30 °C then fast cooling to 4 °C promotes the network) ([ResearchGate — monoglycerides & partial coalescence](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/365674363_Partial_coalescence_and_whipping_capabilities_of_dairy_aerated_emulsions_as_affected_by_inclusion_of_monoglycerides_with_different_fatty_acid_species)).
- Starbucks' **sweet cream cold foam** (heavy cream + 2% milk + syrup, frothed cold) is a hybrid: enough protein for film stabilization, enough cold solid fat for partial-coalescence body. This is why it's denser and longer-lived than plain nonfat cold foam.

### Casein vs whey
- Caseins adsorb preferentially over whey proteins at dairy interfaces and dominate the air–serum interface of milk foams; they are the primary stabilizing species ([J. Dairy Sci. — interfacial material of skim milk foams](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S002203021100258X), [PDF](https://www.journalofdairyscience.org/article/S0022-0302(11)00258-X/pdf)).
- β-casein is highly surface active (flexible, amphiphilic, no rigid tertiary structure) and adsorbs fast even cold — key reason milk foams at fridge temperature at all, since whey proteins stabilize better once heat-denatured (not available in a cold process).
- Raising total protein 1.5→4.0% significantly increases foam stability; shifting casein:whey ratio from 80:20 to 40:60 substantially *decreases* stability ([ScienceDirect — Zhao et al.](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S095869462030128X); [Borcherding 2009, Int. J. Dairy Tech. — protein content, casein–whey ratio and pH effects](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1471-0307.2009.00472.x)). **Formulation lever: fortify with micellar casein/MPC, not WPC, for a cold-whipped product.**

### Fat: destabilizer below ~40 °C, contributor above ~30–40% fat
- At intermediate fat levels (whole milk, half-and-half), liquid/semi-solid fat is the enemy of cold foam: fat globules compete with proteins for the interface; spread liquid fat ruptures lamellae; intact cold globules with fat crystals can puncture bubbles ([Miller's Bio Farm — frothing science](https://millersbiofarm.com/blog/the-science-behind-milk-frothing-why-do-some-froth-better-than-others); [Twisted Goat](https://twistedgoatcoffee.com/blogs/recipes/how-to-make-cold-foam-without-heavy-cream)).
- Skim milk powder dispersions at 0.5% protein gave ~16× the foam volume of whole-milk dispersions ([cf. Springer Food Bioprocess Technol.](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11947-024-03407-y)).
- Skim milk (~3.4 g protein/100 mL, ~0.1% fat) is the benchmark cold-foaming liquid; heavy cream (~5 g protein/cup but 88 g fat/cup) works via the fat-crystal mechanism instead. The "dead zone" is ~3–15% fat — too much fat to protein-film-stabilize, too little to build a fat network. **Design around either <1% fat + high protein, or >28% fat, or blend (Starbucks approach).**

### Collapse mechanisms (all three act simultaneously and synergistically)
([FSTDESK — foam drainage & coalescence](https://fstdesk.com/articles/foam-drainage-and-coalescence); [Springer — foaming/defoaming review](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s43938-023-00025-6); [Recent developments in food foams](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1359029420300996))
1. **Drainage** — gravity + capillary suction pulls serum out of lamellae/Plateau borders; films thin, foam dries top-down, then ruptures. First and fastest mechanism in cold foam (visible liquid layer under foam within minutes). Countered by continuous-phase viscosity (thickeners) and small bubbles.
2. **Coalescence** — rupture of thin films between adjacent bubbles → fewer, bigger bubbles → coarse texture, then collapse. Countered by strong viscoelastic interfacial films (protein, protein–polysaccharide complexes).
3. **Ostwald ripening / disproportionation** — gas diffuses from small (high Laplace pressure) to large bubbles per the Laplace–Young law. Fast in food foams because air/CO₂ are soluble in water; N₂ is far less soluble, which is why nitrogen foams (nitro cans) coarsen dramatically slower. Countered by high-surface-modulus interfacial layers (proteins, particles) and insoluble gas ([ResearchGate — control of Ostwald ripening via high surface modulus surfactants](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/51779890_Control_of_Ostwald_Ripening_by_Using_Surfactants_with_High_Surface_Modulus); [Foam Ripening](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/230545496_Foam_Ripening)).

### Temperature
- Skim milk foam *stability* peaks around 45 °C (casein-driven peak); foamability is good cold but foams made at 5 °C drain faster; minimal drainage reported in the 30–40 °C window in one study, most stable skim foams at 50–60 °C in another ([Kamath et al. — influence of temperature on the foaming of milk](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0958694608000873); [Effect of foaming temperature on skimmed milk](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0958694607002403); [Temperature effect on foamability/stability/structure of milk](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0927775714001770)).
- BUT cold frothing packs more, smaller, denser bubbles (higher serum viscosity, no fat melting): "frothing while milk is still cold produces a creamier, longer-lasting, more billowy foam" ([America's Test Kitchen frothing guide](https://www.americastestkitchen.com/how_tos/6454-frothing-extra-foamy-milk)). Cold serum viscosity slows drainage; cold solid fat in cream enables partial coalescence. Net: cold foam should be made and held at 2–5 °C; it deteriorates faster if it warms on a room-temp drink.
- Real-world hold: unstabilized cold foam holds structure ~5–10 min on an iced drink; sweetened/stabilized versions ~15 min; ideal air phase for pourable-but-floating cold foam ≈ 40–60 vol% air ([Overclock Coffee cold foam guide](https://overclockcoffee.com/blogs/blog/what-is-cold-foam-how-to-make-it)).
- Sugar increases serum viscosity and slows drainage (why sweet cream foam outlasts plain), but high sugar slightly suppresses initial foamability (see §7 dulce section for numbers).

### Proteins (the foam formers)
- **Micellar casein / MPC / sodium caseinate**: primary interfacial stabilizer; increasing protein 1.5→4% markedly improves stability ([Borcherding](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1471-0307.2009.00472.x)). Sodium caseinate ~0.5–2% is standard in non-dairy creamers; Coffee-Mate's cold foam uses micellar casein specifically (better cold foaming than caseinate salts).
- **Whey proteins**: good foamability but weaker cold-foam stability vs casein; heat pre-denaturation improves them (applicable in *processing* the product before packing, not cold in-cup).
- **Egg white powder**: overrun ~280–400%; strong films, but flavor/allergen issues in a dairy creamer.
- **Aquafaba**: overrun 180–475% (canned chickpea), up to 832% fresh, spray-dried microgels 709–963%; +1% guar gum boosts stability; peas/chickpeas best (20–25%), soy worst (5%) — the vegan option for a plant-based SKU ([ScienceDirect — aquafaba as egg white substitute](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0268005X18321349); [MDPI Foods 2023](https://www.mdpi.com/2304-8158/12/4/775); [Springer — aquafaba microgels](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11947-023-03231-w)).
- **Gelatin**: sets cold foam semi-permanently (marshmallow direction) — wrong texture for pourable cold foam, but sub-0.2% can slow drainage.

### Small-molecule emulsifiers (fat-containing / aerosol systems)
- **Mono-/diglycerides**: displace protein from fat globule surfaces → controlled partial coalescence during aeration = faster whip, stiffer foam ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/365674363_Partial_coalescence_and_whipping_capabilities_of_dairy_aerated_emulsions_as_affected_by_inclusion_of_monoglycerides_with_different_fatty_acid_species)). Present in both International Delight and Coffee-Mate cold foam cans.
- **Polysorbate 60/65**: US limit 0.4% in whipped toppings; preferred combos PS60+PS65 (PS65 0.03–0.19%) or PS60:sorbitan monostearate ≈ 2–3:1; water-soluble protein 0.3–0.8% in those patents ([21 CFR 172.836](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-172/subpart-I/section-172.836); [US5384145 low-fat whipped topping](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/5384145)).
- **Sucrose esters**: nonionic, work cold, used in non-dairy whipping creams/toppings; alpha-tending emulsifiers (LMG, PGMS) build stiff aerosol foams ([ScienceDirect — food emulsifier overview](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/pharmacology-toxicology-and-pharmaceutical-science/food-emulsifier)).
- **Sodium stearoyl lactylate (SSL)**: whipping promoter (in ID cold foam creamer).
- All above are FSANZ-permitted additive classes in Australia (Standard 1.3.1; carrageenan INS 407, xanthan INS 415, gellan INS 418, etc.) — verify Schedule 15 category limits for dairy-based drinks/creamers during formulation ([FSANZ additives](https://www.foodstandards.gov.au/consumer/additives/additivecontrol); [FSANZ Code](https://www.foodstandards.gov.au/sites/default/files/2023-12/P1025-Code-Revision-AppR-Attach-C.pdf)).

## 4. Quality Retention Over Shelf Life

### Why foamability degrades in storage
- **Lipolysis is the #1 foam killer.** Psychrotrophic bacteria (esp. *Pseudomonas*) in cold-stored raw milk secrete heat-stable lipases; liberated free fatty acids (FFAs) are potent surface-active foam breakers that displace/weaken protein films. FFA level correlates directly with lost foam capacity and stability; fix = low-FFA raw milk, minimal raw-milk cold-storage time, rapid pasteurization ([PMC — improving foaming of pasteurized milk](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11854643/); [UHT spoilage by Pseudomonas lipases/proteases, PhD Melbourne](https://www.academia.edu/4253091/UHT_milk_spoilage_owing_to_lipases_and_proteases_from_psychrotrophic_Pseudomonas_species_Characteristics_of_enzymatic_breakdown_improved_assays_for_enzyme_detection_and_sources_of_the_bacteria_PhD_Melbourne_2008_)). **Spec raw milk on FFA/ADV and total psychrotroph count.**
- **Proteolysis / age gelation (UHT):** residual plasmin (survives UHT) hydrolyzes β-/αs-caseins over months → bitterness, viscosity rise, gelation (observed from ~10 weeks in susceptible milk); shelf-life-limiting for UHT dairy; worsened by direct-steam (gentler) UHT which inactivates less plasmin ([Chavan et al., Compr. Rev. Food Sci. Food Saf. 2011](https://ift.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1541-4337.2011.00157.x); [ScienceDirect — plasmin & age gelation](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0958694613003099)).
- **Fat destabilization in stored whipping creams:** creaming, clustering/rebodying and free-fat formation during static refrigerated storage reduce whippability and foam stiffness over life ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0268005X22001333)).
- Milk foaming properties measurably change over storage ([Foaming properties and foam structure of milk during storage](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/327182664_Foaming_properties_and_foam_structure_of_milk_during_storage)).

### Heat treatment options vs foam performance
- **HTST (72–75 °C/15 s):** ~7–14 days refrigerated. Freshest flavor; foam closest to raw; shortest retail window.
- **ESL (high-heat ~125–135 °C/1–4 s, or microfiltration 0.8–1.4 µm ceramic membranes + pasteurization):** **21–45 days refrigerated (claims to 90)** — the sweet spot for a branded refrigerated creamer; MF-ESL also strips spores/somatic cells ([Hoffmann 2006, Int. J. Dairy Tech.](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1471-0307.2006.00275.x); [PMC — optimum ESL thermal processing](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5704146/); [Lorenzen 2011 — ESL vs HTST vs UHT survey](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1471-0307.2010.00656.x)). ESL spoilage is mainly psychrotolerant spore-formers/recontamination — filling hygiene critical ([PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5281617/)).
- **UHT (135–150 °C/2–5 s):** months ambient. Whey denaturation from UHT *helps* cold foaming: at low temperature UHT milk is more foamable than pasteurized; UHT 3.5% fat gave the most stable, smallest-bubble, most homogeneous foam at 40 °C in KRÜSS DFA testing; below 30 °C UHT vs pasteurized differ considerably ([KRÜSS AR274](https://www.kruss-scientific.com/en/know-how/application-reports/ar274-foamability-of-different-milk-types-and-stability-and-structure-of-the-foam-produced); [Kamath et al.](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0958694608000873)). Downsides: cooked note + plasmin age-gelation ceiling (~6–9 months practical).
- **Homogenization:** two-stage 10–30 MPa reduces globules 1–8 µm → 0.3–0.8 µm; smaller globules markedly improve whole-milk foamability/stability in several studies (fat crystals too small to rupture lamellae; caseins coat new fat surface), though some studies found only marginal effects — membrane composition matters as much as size ([ScienceDirect — native fat globule size & foaming](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0260877420303198); [homogenisation pressure & aerosol whipping cream](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0958694622000164); [Kamath homogenisation study](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0023643807003970)). Caveat: for a *whipping-cream-mechanism* product, over-homogenization kills whippability (needs clusterable fat) — homogenize the milk phase, gently or not at all the cream phase, or rely on emulsifiers.
- **Cold chain:** refrigerated product must hold ≤4–5 °C end-to-end (Queensland summer = real risk); temperature abuse accelerates creaming, lipolysis, spore outgrowth.

### Separation & "shake before use"
- Fluid-gel stabilizers (gellan/carrageenan at §2 rates) prevent creaming/sedimentation at rest; dipotassium phosphate buffers casein against coffee-acid feathering; ID/Coffee-Mate still instruct **"shake well before use"** to redisperse cream lines and re-dissolve gas — design the label instruction in, don't fight it.
- Syneresis (serum leakage) in the dispensed foam is the visible quality failure; whipped-topping QC tracks height change ratio + water syneresis over time ([US9295275 freeze-thaw-stable topping patent](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/9295275)).
- Shelf-life anchors: refrigerated liquid creamers **7–14 days after opening** (USDA ~2 weeks) ([CAFELY](https://cafely.com/blogs/info/how-long-does-coffee-creamer-last); [The Takeout](https://www.thetakeout.com/1988530/how-long-open-creamer-good-open/)); Rich's frozen RTU foam 365 d frozen / 21 d thawed-refrigerated; aerosol N₂O cans typically multi-week refrigerated codes; UHT ambient 6–9 months.
- **Powder option** (longest life): gas-injected spray-dried foaming creamers (cappuccino tech) trap N₂ in low-density particles (bulk density 0.10–0.20 g/cc vs 0.40–0.60 normal) that release gas on reconstitution — 12–24-month ambient sachet ([US6129943 gasified-carbohydrate foaming creamer](https://patents.google.com/patent/US6129943); [powdered creamer ~2 y unopened](https://lifeboostcoffee.com/blogs/lifeboost/coffee-creamer-shelf-life-does-coffee-creamer-go-bad)). Cold-water-foaming versions are technically hard (most such powders target hot drinks) — would need cold-soluble proteins + methylcellulose.

## 5. Measurable Quality Specs & Realistic QC

### Industry metrics
- **Overrun %** = (M₁ − M₂)/M₂ × 100, M₁ = mass of fixed volume of un-aerated liquid, M₂ = mass of same volume of foam (alt. volumetric: ((V·ρ/weight) − 1)×100) ([US9295275](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/9295275); [US11445734 cream compositions for foaming systems](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/11445734)). Targets: pourable cold foam ~65–150% (40–60 vol% air); siphon foam 200–300%; whipped topping 200–400%.
- **Foam half-life / drainage time**: time for 50% of incorporated liquid to drain back (weigh foam in a funnel over a tared vessel; record time to 50% of initial liquid mass) ([standardized foaming-properties collaborative study](https://www.academia.edu/27682450/Standardized_Procedure_for_Measuring_Foaming_Properties_of_Three_Proteins_A_Collaborative_Study); [FSTDESK](https://fstdesk.com/articles/foam-drainage-and-coalescence)). Alternative: foam-height half-life. Spec suggestion: ≥10 min drainage half-life at 5 °C for "premium" claim; benchmark competitors in the same jig.
- **Bubble size distribution**: KRÜSS DFA100 + Foam Structure Module does automated image analysis — bubble count/mm², mean radius ± SD tracked over decay ([KRÜSS DFA100](https://www.kruss-scientific.com/en/products-services/products/dfa100); [ATA Scientific — Australian distributor](https://www.atascientific.com.au/products/dfa100-dynamic-foam-analyser/)). Small-producer proxy: smartphone macro photo of foam against graduated-cylinder wall at t=0/5/10 min + ImageJ; finer, tighter distribution = slower ripening.
- **Viscosity**: unaerated base viscosity is the main drainage-control dial; Brookfield-type or Zahn/Ford flow cup; xanthan/gellan bases are shear-thinning so fix the measurement shear.
- **pH**: fresh dairy base 6.6–6.8; spec ±0.1 and track over life — falling pH signals spoilage; milk foam performance is pH-sensitive ([Borcherding](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1471-0307.2009.00472.x)).
- **Whipped-system QC panel from patent art**: overrun, texture/stiffness, height change ratio, water syneresis over storage ([US9295275](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/9295275)).

### Realistic small-producer QC bench (per batch)
1. Overrun by cup-weight method (scale + fixed 100 mL cup) — 2 min.
2. Drainage half-life: 100 mL foam in funnel over graduated cylinder at 5 °C, time to 50% drained.
3. Foam height retention on a standard iced test drink at 0/5/10/15 min (photo log).
4. Base viscosity (flow cup or portable viscometer), pH meter, °Brix.
5. Micro: standard plate count + psychrotroph count on raw milk intake; ADV/FFA on cream lots (foam-critical, §4); end-of-life organoleptic + foam re-test on retained samples.
6. Shelf-life validation: foam retained samples weekly to end of code — the spec that matters is **overrun and half-life at end of shelf life**, not day 1.
7. **Foam-on-acid challenge** (added for the iced-black kit — see §6).

## 6. Foam Behaviour on Acidic Black Coffee (cold brew / iced long black, pH ~4.8–5.2)

### The acid environment, precisely
- Cold brew pH **4.85–5.13**; hot-brewed 4.85–5.10 — pH nearly identical between methods. The real difference is **titratable acidity**: cold brew can carry up to ~66% less total acid (fewer extracted CQAs/irritant acids), so it delivers less acid load into a foam at the same pH ([Rao & Fuller, *Scientific Reports* 2018](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-018-34392-w); [PMC — acidity & antioxidant activity of cold brew](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6207714/); [PMC — CGA/caffeine in cold brew](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5740146/)). An iced long black (dilute espresso) has similar pH but higher titratable acidity per mL than cold brew — the *worse* case for the foam even though both read "pH ~5".

### Why acid kills casein-stabilized foam (curdling/feathering mechanism)
- Casein's isoelectric point is **pH 4.6**, but visible aggregation starts around **pH ~5.3** — i.e., *inside the cold brew pH range*. As pH approaches the IEP, electrostatic repulsion between casein micelles/adsorbed films collapses; proteins aggregate → feathering flakes, and interfacial films lose integrity ([KR970009036B1 — feathering-stable coffee cream patent](https://patents.google.com/patent/KR970009036B1/en); [BUBS — why creamer curdles](https://www.bubsnaturals.com/blogs/creamers/why-does-my-creamer-curdle-in-my-coffee-understanding-the-science-behind-this-common-issue)).
- Mitigating factors here: contact is **cold** (protein aggregation kinetics at 4–8 °C are far slower — heat + acid together is what shocks proteins into clumping) and only the **bottom face** of the foam touches coffee. Practitioner literature confirms cold brew is much less likely to curdle dairy than hot coffee ([Complete Home Barista](https://completehomebarista.com/guides/why-milk-curdles-coffee-prevent/); [Dripped Coffee](https://drippedcoffee.com/why-does-milk-curdle-in-coffee/)). Expected failure mode: not dramatic curdling but **accelerated basal collapse** — the foam/coffee interface slowly acidifies the lowest lamellae, casein films weaken, bottom bubbles coalesce, foam "melts" into the drink faster than on water/milk.
- Old or slightly soured product is the hidden risk: spoilage lactic acid lowers the base's own pH, so mildly acidic coffee tips it over the aggregation threshold — spec end-of-life pH ≥6.5 (ties to §5).

### Acid-tolerance formulation levers (ranked)
1. **Buffer the base**: dipotassium phosphate (~0.1–0.3%, the universal creamer solution) buffers against the coffee's acid pull and sequesters calcium, keeping casein above its precipitation zone — exactly why DKP appears in ID and Coffee-Mate cold foam cans and in "barista edition" milks ([US4341811 fluid non-dairy creamer](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/4341811); [Rosso Coffee — creamer & curdling](https://www.rossocoffeeroasters.com/en-us/blogs/rosso-journal/creamer-curdling)). Trisodium citrate is the cleaner-label alternative.
2. **Fat as armour**: higher-fat systems resist acid curdling because fat globules physically shield proteins — heavy cream > half-and-half > milk ([BUBS](https://www.bubsnaturals.com/blogs/creamers/why-does-my-creamer-curdle-in-my-coffee-understanding-the-science-behind-this-common-issue)). Supports the sweet-cream hybrid architecture over a pure skim-protein foam for this kit.
3. **Acid-stable surface-active hydrocolloids**: **PGA** — surface-active, foam-positive, stable pH 2–10, proven head-retention in acidic beer at 40–100 mg/kg ([Cape Crystal](https://www.capecrystalbrands.com/blogs/cape-crystal-brands/enhancing-beer-quality-with-propylene-glycol-alginate-a-brewers-secret-ingredient)). **Methylcellulose/HPMC** foams are non-ionic → pH-insensitive ([Amazing Food Made Easy](https://www.amazingfoodmadeeasy.com/info/modernist-ingredients/more/methocel-f50)). **Pectin** at pH ~4–5 is soluble and stabilizing (why Starbucks' RTD nitro sweet cream uses pectin, not carrageenan — carrageenan loses function/precipitates as pH drops).
4. **Slow interface mixing**: a gellan fluid-gel foam with yield stress drains less liquid into the coffee and admits less coffee into the foam — density contrast plus yield stress keeps a sharp layer. Pour-order instruction on the kit (foam last, over the back of a spoon or via dispenser) materially extends layer life.
5. **QC (new test)**: **foam-on-acid challenge** — standard foam dose onto pH-4.8 citrate-adjusted water (or Tarte cold brew reference batch) at 5 °C; record layer half-life and flake formation vs water control. Spec: no visible feathering at 15 min; layer half-life on coffee ≥70% of half-life on water.

## 7. Flavoured Variants: Vanilla Bean, Dulce de Leche, Matcha

### Vanilla bean (lowest risk)
- Vanillin is documented as an **antifoam agent** in bioprocessing, and flavour compounds in oil/ethanol carriers are classic foam breakers (spreading oil ruptures lamellae) ([Springer — foam control in biotech](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s43938-023-00039-0); [Barista Hustle — anti-foams](https://www.baristahustle.com/lesson/ms-3-03-anti-foams/)). At beverage doses (~0.05–0.2% extract) the effect is minor, but: **use water/propylene-glycol-based or spray-dried vanilla flavour, not oleoresin/oil-based**, and validate overrun vs unflavoured control. Vanilla bean seeds are inert, wettable specks — cosmetic only.
- Vanillin oxidises to vanillic acid in fresh/pasteurised milk (flavour fade over shelf life) but is stable in most other dairy matrices — flavour-dose for end-of-code, not day 1 ([ScienceDirect — vanillin oxidation in dairy](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0308814696002993)).
- Vanilla syrup/sugar is foam-positive at moderate levels (below).

### Dulce de leche / caramelized sugar-milk (moderate risk, manageable)
- Sugar is a two-sided dial: dissolved sucrose **reduces foamability** (viscosity fights air incorporation) but **increases foam stability** (slower drainage, stronger lamellae; foam half-life rises with sugar via increased continuous-phase viscosity/fluid-gel yield stress). Optimum ~**10% sugar** in whey-protein model foams; **15% was detrimental** to overrun, stability and rheology ([MDPI — sucrose in WPI meringues](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/11/11/4764); [ScienceDirect — sugars in agar fluid-gel foams](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0268005X18302728); [ScienceDirect — sucrose in EWP/WPI foams](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0268005X09002045)). **Formulate the dulce variant to ≤10–12% total sugars in the whipped base; expect ~10–20% lower overrun than vanilla; compensate with longer froth time or +0.01% gellan.**
- Maillard-glycated proteins from dulce generally *improve* protein surface activity and film cohesion (mild positive), but caramel raises density — too much makes foam sink or streak into the coffee layer. Keep flavour paste ≤8–10% of base; re-check foam density vs the "must float on ~1.02–1.04 g/mL coffee" requirement.
- Caramelization lowers pH slightly (Maillard acids) and added dairy solids raise calcium — both nudge toward the casein aggregation zone on acid coffee; carry the DKP/citrate buffer in this SKU especially.

### Matcha (highest risk — three simultaneous effects)
1. **Polyphenol–protein binding (net positive for foam)**: EGCG and other catechins are surface-active and complex with milk proteins via hydrophobic/H-bonding; in matcha itself, polyphenols improve foaming capacity (EGCG raised foaming ability 1.89-fold) while amino acids (theanine/valine) stabilize the foam ([ScienceDirect — matcha foam formation mechanisms](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0308814624036598)). In milk–tea systems, protein binds 20→51% of total phenols as protein rises (affinity β-casein > α-casein > β-LG), and polyphenol–protein complexes can *reinforce* interfacial films ([J. Dairy Sci. — milk-tea model](https://www.journalofdairyscience.org/article/S0022-0302(24)01115-9/fulltext); [Food Hydrocolloids — tea polyphenol/milk protein review](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0268005X17313504)). Net: a matcha-in-cream foam usually whips at least as well as plain — practitioner recipes confirm cream+milk+matcha froths to ~3× volume ([Emily Laurae](https://emilylaurae.com/matcha-cold-foam/); [Nio Teas](https://nioteas.com/blogs/matcha/matcha-cold-foam)). Trade-off: bound catechins mute bitterness/astringency (sensorially fine) but blunt "antioxidant" marketing claims.
2. **Particulates**: matcha is a 5–20 µm leaf-particle suspension. Well-wetted hydrophilic particles are foam-neutral-to-positive (quasi-Pickering + serum viscosity); the danger is **clumps/poorly wetted powder**, which act like bridging antifoam particles — a partially hydrophobic particle bridging a lamella dewets and ruptures it ([antifoam mechanisms](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/46027702_The_Mechanism_of_Action_of_Antifoams); [ScienceDirect — hydrophobic-particle antifoaming](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0021979784712884)). Practitioner data: sifting matcha boosts smooth integration 80–90%; unsifted matcha has ~70% clump failure rate ([ODIFORGO matcha cold foam guide](https://odiforgo-kap.com/blogs/barista-hub/matcha-cold-foam)). **Manufacturing fix: pre-disperse matcha under high shear (or use micronized/extract grade), never dry-dose; sieve/mesh check on the batch.**
3. **Sedimentation over shelf life**: matcha particles settle and compact (classic matcha-latte problem); settled sludge also concentrates at a can's dip tube. This variant *needs* the fluid-gel suspension system — high-acyl gellan ~0.02–0.05% (same mechanism as cocoa suspension in chocolate milk with κ-carrageenan 0.01–0.035%, §2) — plus "shake well" labelling. Matcha chlorophyll degrades (browns) with acid, light, heat: opaque packaging, tight cold chain; expect shorter organoleptic code (~2/3 of vanilla SKU pending trials).
- Matcha catechins slightly lower base pH and add polyphenol–protein aggregation pressure on acid contact; keep the buffer system in this SKU and run the §6 acid challenge per variant.

### Variant QC addendum
Run every flavour variant through the full §5 panel *against the unflavoured control*: overrun delta (accept within −20%), drainage half-life (accept ≥ control), foam-on-acid challenge (§6), plus variant-specific checks — sediment height after 7/14/21 d at 4 °C (matcha), colour drift ΔE (matcha), density/float check (dulce), flavour retention at end of code (vanilla).

## Formulation Synthesis & Product-Format Takeaways

**Base architecture for the iced-black kit** (refrigerated "froth-it-yourself" bottle): sweet-cream hybrid (cream + skim milk to ~8–12% fat, ≥3% protein, optionally +0.5–1% MPC/micellar casein), sugars ≤10–12%, **high-acyl gellan 0.02–0.04% fluid gel** (or xanthan 0.05–0.1%), **dipotassium phosphate or trisodium citrate 0.1–0.3% buffer — non-optional given acidic black coffee contact**, optional PGA 0.05–0.1% or pectin for acid-facing film reinforcement (prefer pectin/PGA over carrageenan in this acid-contact use case), mono-/diglycerides 0.1–0.3% if a stiffer whip is wanted. Water-based vanilla flavour; matcha pre-dispersed and gellan-suspended; dulce capped on total sugars and density-checked for float. ESL processing (21–45 d code), FFA-spec'd milk intake, opaque pack, "shake well, froth 20 s with any frother, pour over the iced black last."

**Format options ranked:**
1. **Refrigerated froth-at-home bottle** (Chobani-style) — lowest capex, leverages café brand, needs MF/ESL co-packing.
2. **N₂O self-foaming can** (ID/Coffee-Mate architecture) — best consistency, zero equipment; needs aerosol dairy co-packer.
3. **Siphon program** — branded ≥28% fat sweet-cream base for customers' iSi whippers (2% w/w N₂O charge); premium niche.
4. **Nitro widget can** — a full RTD drink play (pectin + nitrogen, Starbucks template), not a topping.
5. **Powder sachet** (gasified spray-dried creamer) — ambient long-life but technically hardest for *cold* reconstitution.
